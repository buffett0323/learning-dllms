Parallel decoding (P05) commits several tokens per step and accepts some risk. There are two ways to commit fast and fix mistakes afterwards: let another model check the draft, or let the dLLM revise its own committed tokens.

## A dLLM drafts, an AR head verifies

Speculative decoding for AR models drafts a few tokens cheaply, then the target model checks them all in one forward pass and keeps the longest prefix it agrees with. A block diffusion model is a natural drafter: one call proposes a whole block.

Nemotron-Labs-Sigma ([notes](../../papers/sigma/)) and NLD do this inside **one** model. Both are trained with an AR loss next to the diffusion loss (M12), so the same backbone has a causal AR head. One round:

1. **Draft**: one diffusion call from pure noise proposes a block of 32 tokens. Sigma uses its distilled few-step model, so one call is enough.
2. **Verify**: the AR head scores the block in one causal pass and keeps the longest prefix that matches its own greedy tokens, plus one token of its own.

Two forward passes commit the accepted length plus one. On one GB200, batch 1, the 8B models accept 7–12 tokens per round and run 2.4–4.2× faster than their own AR decoding (Sigma Fig. 9).

What it guarantees: the output **is** the AR head's greedy output. So the accuracy is that of AR mode, not of diffusion mode, and diffusion only buys speed. For Sigma-8B that is 67.48 instead of 71.16 on its base benchmarks (Sigma Table 2).

## The dLLM edits its own tokens

The other way keeps the dLLM in charge. Under absorbing noise a committed token is final (M02); LLaDA 2.1 lifts that. Each step can unmask positions above $\tau_{\text{mask}}$ and also replace committed tokens the model now disagrees with, above $\tau_{\text{edit}}$. A low $\tau_{\text{mask}}$ drafts fast and relies on editing (M06).

Here nothing outside the dLLM checks the result, so the output keeps diffusion-mode quality, but there is no guarantee to point to.
